## Executive overview

Seller financing generally means the seller accepts a contractual right to receive part of the purchase price after closing. The buyer signs a note, and payments are made under agreed terms. It can help bridge a financing gap, align confidence in the business, or make a transition more feasible. It also means the seller remains exposed to the buyer, the business, the capital structure, and the enforceability of the documents.

This guide describes educational structures only. It does not recommend terms, predict collectability, or replace legal, tax, accounting, credit, or investment review.

## The basic structure

A seller note normally identifies principal, interest, payment schedule, maturity, default provisions, remedies, and the parties obligated. It may be secured or unsecured, guaranteed or not, subordinated to senior financing, and combined with buyer equity, bank debt, earnout, rollover equity, or retained assets.

Model the full funds flow:

- Purchase consideration and assumed liabilities
- Buyer cash equity
- Senior acquisition debt
- Seller-note principal
- Earnout or contingent consideration
- Rollover equity
- Working capital and transaction expenses
- Debt payoff and seller cash at close

Do not call deferred value “cash at closing.” Compare timing, risk, control, security, tax treatment, and dependencies.

## Payment terms, interest, and amortization

The note can require level payments, interest-only periods, customized schedules, or other professionally designed terms. Amortization determines the payment calculation; maturity determines when the remaining balance is due. If maturity comes before full amortization, a balloon payment may remain.

A lower periodic payment can increase the balloon and refinance risk. A faster schedule can pressure business cash flow. Interest, default interest, prepayment, late fees, and payment application need precise drafting and professional review.

Use explicit assumptions when modeling. Do not invent a rate, term, or payment that appears to be available from a lender or acceptable to either party.

## Security and subordination

Security may include a lien on business assets, equity interests, specific property, or other collateral, subject to law, existing liens, and negotiated documents. A personal guarantee may provide another claim but is not the same as liquid collateral or assured collection.

Senior lenders often require the seller note to be subordinated. Subordination can restrict payments, remedies, lien priority, amendments, or enforcement while senior debt is outstanding. The seller should understand exactly what actions are blocked, when payments can stop, and what information rights remain.

Ask counsel to review lien searches, perfection, intercreditor terms, guarantees, covenants, and enforcement. A signed note without a practical recovery path may provide less protection than the headline balance suggests.

## Buyer equity and qualification

Buyer equity demonstrates capital at risk and affects the overall leverage. The source, availability, timing, and conditions should be documented. A seller should not rely only on a buyer’s stated liquidity.

Evaluate the buyer within an authorized, confidential process:

- Relevant operating and leadership experience
- Acquisition and integration experience
- Documented equity and financing status
- Personal and business obligations
- Proposed management plan
- Understanding of cash-flow variability
- References and professional support
- Integrity of supplied information

Qualification does not mean approval or guarantee. It creates a better evidence basis for a seller decision.

## Cash flow and default risk

The business may need to fund senior debt, seller-note payments, working capital, taxes, capital expenditures, and the buyer’s compensation. Stress-test the structure using clearly labeled assumptions. Consider revenue decline, customer loss, margin compression, delayed collections, employee departure, unexpected capital needs, and a slower transition.

Default risk includes more than missed payments. The buyer may breach reporting, insurance, asset-sale, additional-debt, or operating covenants. Remedies can be costly and uncertain. The seller’s risk tolerance should be considered alongside desired price and timing.

## Due diligence works both ways

The buyer conducts diligence on the business; a financing seller conducts diligence on the buyer and structure. Request only necessary information through secure, authorized channels.

Review:

- Buyer entity and ownership
- Sources and uses
- Financing commitments and conditions
- Personal financial information where lawful and appropriate
- Background and references
- Operating plan and leadership coverage
- Insurance and collateral
- Senior loan and intercreditor documents
- Closing conditions and post-close reporting

Coordinate the request with counsel and financing professionals. Do not retain unnecessary sensitive information.

## Documentation and administration

The transaction may require a note, purchase agreement provisions, security agreement, guarantees, subordination or intercreditor agreement, disclosure schedules, escrow instructions, and servicing arrangements. The appropriate package is transaction-specific.

After closing, define where payments go, who maintains the ledger, how statements are delivered, what financial reporting is required, how notices are sent, and who handles disputes. Calendar maturity, reporting, insurance, and covenant dates.

Read [What Happens After Closing](/resources/what-happens-after-closing) before treating closing as the end of the seller’s obligations.

## Compare seller financing with other consideration

Evaluate the note beside cash, earnout, escrow, holdback, and rollover equity. Each has different triggers and risk.

A seller note is a debt claim under its documents. An earnout depends on future performance and measurement rules. Rollover equity depends on the value, governance, and liquidity of the continuing investment. Escrow or holdback may secure specified obligations. Do not combine these into one “deferred proceeds” line.

Create a comparison showing:

- Amount and expected timing
- Conditions to payment
- Priority and security
- Information and control rights
- Dependence on business performance
- Tax and accounting review
- Default or dispute process
- Estimated collection expenses and complexity

## Negotiate reporting before closing

A seller carrying credit may need periodic financial statements, compliance certificates, insurance evidence, notice of defaults, budgets, tax returns, or access to specified records. The buyer needs reasonable operating flexibility and protection of sensitive information.

Define the exact report, frequency, deadline, preparation standard, recipient, confidentiality, and remedy for non-delivery. Broad inspection rights can create conflict; rights that are too narrow can leave the seller blind.

## Plan for changes after closing

Address additional debt, asset sales, distributions, ownership changes, mergers, relocation, key-person departure, or changes to the business model. The proper covenants depend on the structure and professional advice.

Also consider death, disability, divorce, insolvency, and succession of the buyer or seller. Review insurance, estate, guarantee, assignment, and notice implications with qualified professionals.

## Marketability and negotiation

Seller financing can expand the pool of feasible structures, but it can also signal risk if offered without limits. Decide in advance the maximum exposure, acceptable security, payment priority, duration, information rights, and events that make seller financing unacceptable.

Do not advertise terms the seller has not approved. Present openness as conditional on buyer qualification, diligence, structure, documents, and professional review.

## Questions to ask advisors

- How does the note interact with senior financing?
- What collateral and guarantees are legally and practically available?
- Which payments or remedies can be blocked by subordination?
- What tax treatment and timing need review?
- What reporting and covenant package is appropriate?
- Who services the note and maintains the payment record?
- What happens after default, sale of the business, death, disability, or insolvency?
