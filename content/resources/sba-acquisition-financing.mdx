## Executive overview

SBA-backed financing can support eligible business acquisitions through loans made by participating lenders and supported by an SBA guaranty. The lender—not Seller Transition Exchange—underwrites the request, applies current program requirements, and decides whether to proceed. SBA identifies complete and partial changes of ownership as permitted uses of 7(a) loan proceeds on its current [7(a) program page](https://www.sba.gov/funding-programs/loans/7a-loans).

Program rules, lender policies, forms, fees, and procedures change. This guide intentionally avoids quoting current limits, required percentages, or universal eligibility rules. Confirm current requirements with the lender and the SBA’s governing [SOP 50 10 materials](https://www.sba.gov/document/sop-50-10-lender-development-company-loan-programs).

## Understand the roles

The buyer is generally the borrower or controls the borrowing entity. The lender evaluates the buyer, business, transaction, repayment ability, eligibility, structure, collateral or guarantees where applicable, documentation, and closing conditions. SBA provides program rules and a guaranty to the lender; it does not eliminate lender underwriting or buyer obligations.

The seller supplies accurate business and transaction information, supports diligence, resolves documentation gaps, and negotiates seller obligations. Advisors handle legal, tax, accounting, valuation, and financing questions within their professional roles.

## Buyer equity and capital sources

Acquisition structures commonly combine buyer equity with debt and may include seller financing or other sources. The required equity treatment depends on current program rules, lender policy, transaction facts, and the buyer’s source of funds.

Document the source, availability, timing, and conditions for each dollar. Avoid counting borrowed, contingent, or unavailable funds as buyer equity without lender review. Identify working capital and transaction expenses separately from purchase consideration.

## Cash flow and debt service

Lenders examine whether cash flow supports required obligations under their underwriting method. Historical performance, normalized adjustments, owner compensation, replacement management, debt payments, working capital, capital expenditures, and projected changes can affect the analysis.

Prepare a transparent bridge from reported results to any proposed normalized cash flow. Show every adjustment and source. Do not assume a lender accepts seller or buyer add-backs. Stress-test performance under plausible operating pressure, but label scenarios as educational and input-dependent.

## Seller notes

A seller note may be part of the structure, but payment, standby, subordination, maturity, and equity treatment are subject to current rules, lender requirements, and negotiated documents. Do not describe one arrangement as universally required or accepted.

The seller should understand payment restrictions, lien priority, information rights, default remedies, and the buyer’s total leverage. Read [Seller Financing](/resources/seller-financing) and obtain independent legal and tax review.

## Working capital

An acquisition needs enough liquidity to operate after closing. Build a monthly cash view that includes payroll, inventory, receivables, payables, seasonality, deposits, deferred revenue, capital expenditures, and integration costs.

A purchase price that consumes all available capital can leave an otherwise sound business exposed. Ask the lender how working capital is analyzed and documented. Do not assume the historical balance sheet or a seller estimate automatically establishes the need.

## Business and buyer considerations

The lender reviews business eligibility under current SBA rules and its own policies. The buyer’s experience, credit profile, liquidity, ownership, management plan, and other obligations can also matter. Specific conclusions must come from the lender.

Prepare:

- Clear business description and ownership history
- Transaction structure and sources and uses
- Buyer résumé and relevant operating experience
- Business and personal financial information requested by the lender
- Historical and interim financial statements
- Filed tax returns and reconciliations
- Debt, customer concentration, and working-capital schedules
- Purchase agreement or letter of intent when requested
- Business plan, projections, and stated assumptions
- Licenses, leases, contracts, and material approvals

Different lenders may request different packages or sequences. Confirm before transmitting sensitive documents.

## Lender review and closing dependencies

Financing often moves through initial screening, application, underwriting, valuation or appraisal work where required, documentation, approval conditions, and closing. The exact sequence varies.

Closing dependencies may include:

- Satisfactory diligence and underwriting
- Current financial information
- Executed transaction documents
- Required valuation or appraisal
- Lease, license, franchise, landlord, or third-party approvals
- Insurance
- Lien searches and payoff information
- Buyer equity verification
- Seller-note and subordination documents
- Entity formation and authority

No timeline should be treated as guaranteed. Maintain a dependency tracker with owner, due date, status, and blocking issue.

## Why a financing process may stall

Common causes include inconsistent financial records, unsupported adjustments, insufficient cash flow under the lender’s analysis, unresolved tax or legal issues, weak buyer experience, unavailable equity, changing performance, concentration, transfer restrictions, valuation gaps, incomplete documents, or conditions that cannot be satisfied.

A stall is not always a final denial, but it needs an honest diagnosis. Do not route the same incomplete package repeatedly without resolving the evidence gap.

## Organize the financing request

Use a controlled request record rather than disconnected email attachments. Record the borrower, target business, requested purpose, lender, source documents, observation dates, verification status, open questions, consent to route, and responsible advisor.

Separate:

- Seller-provided business facts
- Buyer-provided personal or entity information
- CPA-prepared or filed records
- Lender assumptions and requests
- Seller Transition Exchange educational calculations
- Missing or conflicting information

Buyer financial information should be visible only to authorized parties with a valid purpose. Seller approval to disclose business records does not authorize disclosure of buyer records, and buyer consent does not override seller confidentiality.

## Prepare projections carefully

If the lender requests projections, connect them to historical performance, signed contracts, staffing, pricing, capacity, working capital, and integration assumptions. Show a base case and clearly labeled sensitivities where appropriate.

Do not use the purchase price, debt capacity, or desired payment as the reason revenue will grow. Explain each assumption, the evidence, the responsible operator, and the date the result is expected. Reconcile projected cash flow to debt service, owner or manager compensation, taxes, capital expenditures, and working capital.

## Maintain source dates

Program pages and SOPs can change. Record the URL, title, effective date where published, review date, and reviewer. Before a financing package is routed or a program statement is relied on, refresh the primary SBA source and confirm the current interpretation with the lender.

## A 30-day readiness plan

1. Ask the lender for its current acquisition checklist and process.
2. Build the sources-and-uses schedule.
3. Reconcile financial statements, returns, and debt.
4. Document buyer experience and management coverage.
5. Identify working-capital and capital-expenditure needs.
6. List every third-party approval and closing dependency.
7. Record current sources and review dates in the deal file.

## Questions to ask a lender

- Which current SBA SOP and lender policies govern this request?
- What documentation is needed for initial review versus underwriting?
- How are cash flow and proposed adjustments analyzed?
- How will buyer equity and seller financing be treated?
- What valuation, appraisal, insurance, or collateral work is expected?
- Which conditions most often delay a transaction like this one?
- Who controls document transmission and status communication?

## Educational boundary

This guide is educational and is not an eligibility determination, loan approval, commitment, rate quote, program interpretation, valuation, or legal or tax advice. Current SBA rules and lender requirements control.
