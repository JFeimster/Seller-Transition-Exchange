## Executive overview

The right advisor team is built around the transaction, not a universal roster. A small asset sale, partner buyout, family succession, leveraged acquisition, and strategic process have different risks. Not every transaction requires every advisor listed here.

Begin with decisions and dependencies. Assign scope, deliverables, authority, confidentiality, compensation, and handoffs. Verify credentials and references rather than assuming a title proves fit.

## Transaction attorney

Transaction counsel can interpret structure, draft and negotiate agreements, coordinate diligence, identify consents, and protect legal positions. Ask about relevant deal size, industry, buyer type, financing, disputes, and who will perform the work day to day.

Clarify whether counsel covers employment, real estate, regulatory, intellectual property, benefits, privacy, or tax matters—or will coordinate specialists.

## CPA and tax advisor

The CPA can help reconcile financial statements, support adjustment schedules, prepare diligence responses, and explain accounting treatment. A tax advisor can analyze structure and timing based on current law and the seller’s facts.

Do not ask either professional to bless a price or structure outside the engagement scope. Confirm whether the firm is performing bookkeeping, compilation, review, audit, tax compliance, transaction support, or quality-of-earnings work; these are different services.

## Broker or M&A advisor

A broker or M&A advisor may help position the business, identify buyers, manage outreach, coordinate process, and negotiate business terms within applicable law and engagement authority. Fit can depend on deal size, geography, industry, buyer network, confidentiality discipline, and licensing requirements.

Review:

- Expected buyer universe and outreach method
- Seller approval controls
- Marketing-material review
- Qualification and NDA process
- Fee, retainer, minimum, tail, and expense terms
- Exclusivity and termination
- Conflicts and buyer representation
- Data ownership and handoff

Do not accept claims of buyer demand without evidence.

## Valuation and quality-of-earnings professionals

A valuation professional provides analysis for a defined purpose, standard, date, and interest. A quality-of-earnings provider examines the composition and sustainability of earnings and related financial issues. One does not automatically replace the other.

Ask what work is appropriate for planning, negotiation, financing, tax, litigation, or fiduciary purposes. Read [Understanding Business Valuation](/resources/understanding-business-valuation).

## Lender and financing advisor

An acquisition lender evaluates a specific financing request under current policy. A financing advisor may organize the request or compare educational structures, depending on scope and applicable requirements.

Ask which transactions the lender handles, what information is needed, how confidentiality is protected, and which conditions commonly delay closing. A financing conversation is not an approval or commitment.

## Wealth, insurance, and exit-planning professionals

A wealth advisor can help the seller model liquidity, concentration, spending, and post-sale priorities. An insurance professional may review transaction, key-person, benefits, or legacy needs. An exit planner may coordinate readiness and personal objectives.

Confirm registration, licensing, compensation, product relationships, custody, conflicts, and the limits of advice. Never infer professional verification from a directory listing.

## Industry and specialist advisors

Some transitions need industry, licensing, environmental, cybersecurity, human resources, benefits, real estate, intellectual-property, or other specialist work. Select the narrowest qualified scope. Industry familiarity is useful only when the advisor distinguishes general practice from company-specific evidence.

## Scope and engagement agreements

Every engagement should identify:

- Client and authorized decision maker
- Exact scope and exclusions
- Deliverables and timeline
- Staffing and responsible partner
- Compensation, expenses, and contingent fees
- Confidentiality and data handling
- Conflicts and consent
- Reliance and intended users
- Termination and file transfer
- Dispute provisions

Read the agreement. Confirm which communications are privileged and which are not with counsel.

## Credentials, references, and conflicts

Verify licenses or registrations with the responsible authority when relevant. Ask for references from comparable work and permission to contact them. Evaluate responsiveness, judgment, plain-language communication, and willingness to state limitations.

Ask directly:

- Who else do you represent or receive compensation from?
- Do you have referral, financing, insurance, or buyer relationships?
- Who owns the work product and data?
- What happens if the transaction changes path?
- Which conclusions require another professional?

## Build an advisor responsibility map

Create one table listing every material decision, responsible advisor, required evidence, deliverable, due date, dependency, and seller approval. Gaps and overlaps become visible before they create delay.

The seller remains the decision maker unless authority is explicitly granted. Advisors should coordinate through approved channels without exposing confidential data beyond their assignment.

## Interview advisors against the work

Use the same fact pattern and questions with each candidate so comparisons are meaningful. Share only the information needed for the interview and protect confidential business details.

Ask candidates to describe:

- A comparable assignment and their exact role
- The first three risks they would investigate
- Work performed by senior versus junior staff
- Expected seller time and information needs
- How they handle disagreement among advisors
- How they protect confidential materials
- What can cause fees or timing to change
- Which outcomes they will not promise

Strong advisors should be able to explain limitations. Treat guarantees, unsupported market-leadership claims, and pressure to sign before conflicts or scope are clear as warning signs.

## Coordinate without creating a committee

Name one transaction coordinator and one seller decision maker. Establish an approved communication channel, issue log, calendar, data-room protocol, and decision record. Advisors should know who owns each question and when another specialty must be involved.

Use short issue statements:

1. Decision required
2. Known facts and sources
3. Missing information
4. Advisor responsible
5. Alternatives and tradeoffs
6. Seller approval
7. Deadline and dependency

This prevents long email chains from becoming undocumented decisions.

## Monitor cost and value

Track fees against scope, deliverables, decisions supported, and risks resolved. Lowest cost is not automatically best, but an open-ended engagement without budget visibility can damage the process.

Ask for updated estimates when scope changes. Review success fees, retainers, credits, minimums, tails, expenses, break fees, and referral compensation. Confirm whether taxes apply and when invoices are due.

Do not let sunk cost force a weak transaction. The seller should retain the ability to pause when diligence, buyer fit, confidentiality, or structure no longer supports the objective.

## A 30-day advisor-selection plan

1. List the next five material decisions.
2. Assign the professional discipline required for each.
3. Prepare a confidential, standardized interview brief.
4. Interview at least two candidates where practical.
5. Verify relevant credentials and references.
6. Review conflicts, compensation, scope, and data handling.
7. Build the responsibility map before work begins.

## Warning signs

Pause when an advisor promises a price, buyer, approval, tax result, or closing; avoids written scope; will not disclose compensation or conflicts; requests broad confidential access without a purpose; or resists coordination with the responsible professional team.

Also examine chronic missed commitments, generic materials that ignore the business, unexplained staff substitutions, and pressure to publish unsupported claims. A credential can establish authority to perform certain work, but it does not prove judgment, capacity, or fit for this assignment.

## Educational boundary

This guide is educational and does not endorse an advisor, credential, service, or compensation arrangement. Licensing, duties, privilege, scope, and conflicts vary. Verify professionals independently and review engagement terms.
