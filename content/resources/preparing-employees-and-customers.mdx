## Executive overview

Employees and customers experience a transaction through daily work: who makes decisions, whether service changes, whether promises remain reliable, and whether leaders appear aligned. Communication should reduce avoidable uncertainty without revealing information before the process is ready.

Timing is transaction-specific. There is no universal rule that every employee or customer should be told at the same stage. Coordinate legal obligations, confidentiality, diligence, financing, and closing conditions with qualified advisors.

## Build the stakeholder map

List people and groups whose departure, concern, or delayed action could affect continuity:

- Senior leaders and key operators
- Licensed or credentialed employees
- Revenue-producing and customer-facing staff
- Concentrated or strategic customers
- Vendors, landlords, platforms, franchisors, and referral partners
- Lenders, bonding, insurance, and regulatory contacts

For each stakeholder, record impact, relationship owner, disclosure risk, required notice or consent, preferred messenger, timing trigger, and follow-up owner.

## Decide confidentiality timing

Use need-to-know access before broad communication. Key employees may need to support diligence or planning, but early disclosure can create retention risk. Obtain legal and transaction advice before requiring confidentiality agreements or changing duties.

Read [Confidential Business Sales](/resources/confidential-business-sales). Keep sensitive information out of casual messaging, calendars, file names, shared drives, and meeting invitations.

## Prepare key employees

Before disclosure, identify role coverage, knowledge gaps, compensation issues, and likely questions. When the time arrives, explain what is known, what is not decided, what remains confidential, and where questions should go.

Retention measures may include role clarity, development, compensation review, transaction-specific incentives, or stay arrangements. These have legal, tax, accounting, benefits, and cultural implications. Do not promise continued employment or terms without authority.

## Sequence employee communication

A useful sequence can include:

1. Authorized leadership and transaction team
2. Key employees needed for planning or diligence
3. Managers who will deliver messages
4. Broader workforce at the agreed trigger
5. Individual follow-up for affected roles

Prepare manager scripts, a question log, escalation rules, and a rumor response. Employees should not learn material news from customers, social media, or an uncontrolled document.

## Plan customer communication

Customer concentration affects timing and risk. A buyer may request introductions before closing, while the seller may need to protect confidentiality and relationships. Decide which customers require consent, notice, diligence contact, or only post-close communication.

The message should address:

- Continuity of service and contacts
- Effective timing
- Leadership and ownership changes
- Contract, billing, or operational changes, if any
- Seller involvement during handoff
- Where questions and concerns go

Avoid guarantees about future performance or staffing. Use accurate, approved statements.

## Introduce the buyer or successor

An introduction should transfer confidence, not only names. Explain the successor’s relevant capability, role, commitment to the business, and how the transition will be supported. The seller should visibly support the agreed authority and avoid undermining it through side conversations.

For important accounts, use a call plan: objectives, participants, known concerns, prohibited topics, commitments, and follow-up. Record feedback and assign actions.

## Preserve brand and service continuity

Define what remains stable on day one: brand, phone numbers, service schedules, support channels, quality standards, billing, and relationship owners. If changes are planned, sequence them around operational capacity.

Create continuity checklists for:

- Open jobs and customer commitments
- Escalations and service recovery
- Pricing and proposal approvals
- Payroll and employee access
- Vendor orders and delivery
- Systems, domains, and phone routing
- Licensing and insurance
- Cash, billing, and collections

## Vendor and partner communication

Review contracts for notice, consent, assignment, or change-of-control requirements. Coordinate messages with counsel. Vendors may change terms, credit, access, or contacts after learning of a transition, so prepare alternatives and confirm critical supplies.

## Prepare for retention decisions

Not every role needs a transaction-specific retention arrangement. Start with business impact, replacement difficulty, knowledge concentration, expected transition work, and the employee’s likely response. Separate ordinary compensation issues from transaction incentives.

For each critical role, consider:

- Responsibilities before, during, and after closing
- Knowledge and relationships that need transfer
- Current performance and career path
- Backup coverage
- Disclosure timing
- Retention risk and available authority
- Legal, tax, payroll, benefits, and accounting review
- What happens if the transaction does not close

Promises made in anxiety can become expensive obligations or damage trust. Use approved written terms and consistent manager communication.

## Build the customer-risk plan

Segment customers by revenue, margin, contract, renewal timing, strategic importance, relationship owner, service sensitivity, and disclosure risk. A large account with an upcoming renewal may need a different sequence from a stable, diversified customer base.

Prepare a risk response for:

- Customer requests to renegotiate
- Concern about service continuity
- Buyer requests for a pre-close reference
- Employee disclosure reaching the customer first
- Confusion over billing or contracts
- A customer that does not want assignment
- Competitor outreach after announcement

Assign one owner to each material relationship and keep commitments in a shared transition log.

## Align seller and buyer behavior

Employees and customers detect disagreement quickly. Before communication, the seller and buyer or successor should agree on roles, language, prohibited promises, escalation, and who has authority at each stage.

The seller should not promise that nothing will change if the buyer has not made that commitment. The buyer should not announce improvements before understanding operating capacity. Both parties should avoid criticizing prior or future leadership.

Rehearse the first employee meeting and the most important customer calls. Prepare difficult questions and honest answers. “That has not been decided; this is who owns the answer and when we will follow up” is better than invented certainty.

## Measure continuity

Set a baseline before communication. Track voluntary turnover, absenteeism, open roles, customer retention, complaints, service levels, on-time delivery, backlog, collections, vendor disruptions, and unresolved questions.

Metrics are signals, not proof of causation. Pair them with direct feedback and documented events. Review the measures weekly during the first month and at agreed intervals through day 100.

## Day-one messaging

Prepare separate messages for employees, customers, vendors, and public channels. Align facts and timing while respecting confidentiality. Every message should answer:

- What happened?
- When is it effective?
- What changes now?
- What stays the same?
- Who is responsible?
- Where do questions go?

Designate one source of truth and update the question log.

## The first 100 days

Communication continues after the announcement. Track employee turnover, attendance, customer feedback, service levels, backlog, complaints, vendor performance, and unresolved commitments.

Use 7-, 30-, 60-, and 100-day reviews. Share what the new leadership has learned, which changes are planned, and which commitments have been completed. Close the loop with people who raised concerns.

## Questions to test the plan

- Who learns what, from whom, and at which trigger?
- Which employees require retention or knowledge-transfer decisions?
- Which customer or vendor contracts require notice or consent?
- What can managers promise?
- How will rumors and accidental disclosure be handled?
- What evidence will show continuity is holding?
