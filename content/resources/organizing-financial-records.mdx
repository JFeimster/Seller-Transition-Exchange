## Executive overview

Financial preparation is not the act of placing years of files into a folder. It is the work of creating a traceable record that explains how the company earns money, uses cash, manages working capital, and supports the adjustments presented to a buyer or lender.

Different buyers, lenders, industries, and transaction structures require different documentation. Do not treat any checklist as a universal request list. Ask the party responsible for diligence or underwriting to confirm scope, period, format, and secure delivery method.

> Clean records shorten expensive conversations.

## Build a controlled document index

Start with an index, not the data room. For every file, record:

- Document category and clear file name
- Covered entity and accounting period
- Original source
- Date obtained
- Responsible owner
- Verification or review status
- Confidentiality level
- Known inconsistency or limitation
- Current version

Preserve original records separately from normalized schedules and summaries. If a number is corrected, document the original, correction, reason, reviewer, and date. Never silently overwrite source evidence.

## Profit-and-loss statements

Prepare monthly and annual profit-and-loss statements for the periods appropriate to the process, plus current interim results. Use consistent accounts and explain material classification changes. Compare periods to identify seasonality, unusual spikes, margin shifts, discontinued work, and new revenue streams.

Build supporting schedules for:

- Revenue by customer, service, product, channel, and location
- Cost of goods or service delivery
- Labor and subcontractor expense
- Gross margin and operating margin
- Owner compensation and related-party expenses
- Nonrecurring or disputed adjustments

If the business uses cash-basis tax reporting but accrual information for operations, explain the difference with the CPA. Buyers and lenders may analyze the records differently.

## Balance sheets and bank records

Balance sheets show the resources and obligations that support the income statement. Reconcile cash, accounts receivable, inventory, fixed assets, accounts payable, credit cards, loans, leases, taxes, and owner accounts.

Provide bank statements only through a secure, approved process and remove unnecessary sensitive data where appropriate. Reconcile statement balances to the books and explain transfers, owner activity, unusual deposits, and outstanding items. A bank statement alone does not prove revenue, and revenue recognition should be reviewed within the business’s accounting framework.

## Tax returns and document consistency

Collect complete filed business tax returns and relevant supporting schedules for the requested periods. Do not alter filed documents. If the management accounts differ from a return, prepare a reconciliation rather than choosing the more favorable number.

Common sources of inconsistency include:

- Different accounting methods or period cutoffs
- Amended returns
- Multiple legal entities
- Personal expenses recorded in the business
- Related-party rent or management fees
- Payroll versus owner distributions
- Revenue recorded net in one system and gross in another
- Unreconciled balance-sheet accounts

Ask the CPA to review material differences. Unsupported explanations can weaken trust even when the underlying issue is manageable.

## Debt schedule

Create a debt schedule that includes lender, borrower, original amount, current balance, interest basis, payment, maturity, collateral, guarantors, liens, covenants, and payoff requirements. Include equipment notes, lines of credit, credit cards, shareholder loans, tax obligations, and other material financing.

Mark disputed balances or missing statements. Do not assume debt will transfer or be paid in a particular manner. Transaction structure, lender consent, and closing documents determine treatment.

## Accounts receivable and accounts payable

Prepare aging schedules that tie to the balance sheet. For receivables, show invoice date, customer, amount, status, disputes, credits, write-off history, and concentration. For payables, show vendor, date, amount, status, disputed items, and past-due obligations.

Analyze:

- Aging trends and collection practices
- Unbilled work or deferred revenue
- Customer credits and refunds
- Bad-debt history
- Vendor concentration and payment terms
- Cutoff errors around month-end
- Related-party or owner balances

Working-capital discussions often rely on normalized operating patterns. One unusual month should not be used without context.

## Payroll and owner compensation

Organize payroll registers, contractor payments, benefits, bonuses, commissions, and owner compensation. Identify the roles performed by owners and related parties. A buyer may need to replace those roles at market cost even if current compensation is unusually high, low, or structured through distributions.

Coordinate employment, tax, benefits, and classification issues with qualified professionals. Do not label a cost as removable merely because it is paid to the owner.

## Add-backs and normalization

Create an adjustment schedule that begins with reported results and shows each proposed adjustment separately. Include the account, amount, period, reason, source evidence, recurring status, and reviewer.

Common discussion categories can include owner compensation normalization, personal expenses, one-time professional fees, nonrecurring repairs, discontinued operations, or unusual events. Acceptance is fact-specific. Buyers and lenders do not necessarily use the same methodology, and no adjustment should be presented as accepted before review.

Read [Understanding Business Valuation](/resources/understanding-business-valuation) for the distinction among SDE, EBITDA, estimates, and formal valuation.

## Inventory and capital expenditures

For inventory, document method, count date, aging, obsolescence, ownership, location, and any consigned goods. Tie totals to the balance sheet. Separate normal operating inventory from excess or obsolete stock.

For fixed assets, prepare an asset register with description, location, acquisition date, cost, depreciation, financing, condition, maintenance, and expected replacement needs. Accounting value is not automatically market value. Identify leased assets, personal property, and items that will not transfer.

## Customer concentration and revenue verification

Prepare customer-level revenue by month and year for the requested period. Use confidential identifiers when names are not yet approved for disclosure. Show percentage of revenue and gross profit, tenure, contract status, renewal terms, owner relationship, and notable changes.

Revenue verification may draw on invoices, contracts, bank activity, payment processor reports, tax records, order systems, and customer schedules. The required combination varies. For digital businesses, include platform statements and account ownership; for service businesses, include job or route records where useful.

## Working capital

Working capital affects operations and transaction economics. Prepare monthly balances and operating metrics for receivables, inventory, payables, deferred revenue, and other relevant accounts. Explain seasonality, growth effects, one-time buildups, and unusual payment timing.

Do not assume a target or peg. Buyers, sellers, accountants, lenders, and attorneys may define working capital differently. Record the definition, included accounts, measurement period, and dispute process in the transaction documents.

## Prepare the data room in stages

Do not grant every interested party full access. Use a structured disclosure path:

1. Seller-approved anonymous information
2. Buyer qualification and confidentiality agreement
3. Controlled overview and summary financials
4. Detailed financial and operating diligence
5. Restricted legal, employee, customer, or personally sensitive records
6. Closing and financing documents for authorized parties

The [Deal Room](/deal-room) should maintain access scope, approval, expiry, revocation, and an audit trail. Confidentiality is a workflow—not a checkbox.

## A 30-day action plan

1. Create the index and source-status fields.
2. Close and reconcile the most recent month.
3. Tie annual statements to filed tax returns or document differences.
4. Build debt, receivable, payable, and adjustment schedules.
5. Identify five unresolved inconsistencies and assign reviewers.
6. Establish disclosure levels before uploading sensitive records.

## A 90-day action plan

1. Complete a consistent monthly close for three consecutive periods.
2. Resolve unexplained balance-sheet accounts.
3. Produce customer and margin analyses that tie to the general ledger.
4. Update inventory, asset, and capital-expenditure schedules.
5. Document working-capital patterns and seasonality.
6. Run a mock buyer and lender document review.
7. Record all remaining limitations and professional-review needs.
