## Executive overview

Business valuation is a disciplined opinion about value for a defined purpose, interest, date, and set of assumptions. It is not one universal number. A value used for internal planning may differ from a conclusion prepared for a partner buyout, estate matter, financing process, or negotiated sale because the purpose, standard, evidence, and rights being valued can differ.

Preliminary estimates can help an owner identify value drivers and planning gaps. They are not appraisals, fairness opinions, tax conclusions, or promises of transaction value. An automated estimate is only as reliable as its inputs and method. Seller Transition Exchange treats value-building outputs as educational until qualified professionals review the relevant evidence.

## Start with the purpose

Ask why the valuation is being considered and who will rely on it. The answer affects the required professional, the scope of work, and the evidence.

- Exit planning and value-gap analysis
- Potential listing-price discussion
- Management, family, or partner buyout
- Financing and lender review
- Tax, estate, gift, or litigation purpose
- Insurance or buy-sell agreement administration
- Negotiation with an identified buyer

Do not use a planning estimate for a purpose that requires an independent valuation. Ask the professional to define the valuation date, ownership interest, standard of value, premise, methods, assumptions, limitations, and intended users.

## Understand SDE and EBITDA

Seller’s discretionary earnings, commonly called SDE, is often discussed for owner-operated businesses. It generally begins with reported earnings and considers owner compensation and selected expenses or adjustments. EBITDA—earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization—is more commonly discussed where management labor is treated as a normalized operating expense.

Neither measure is self-proving. The calculation depends on consistent financial statements and support for each proposed adjustment. A buyer may reject an add-back that is recurring, personal to the buyer’s future plan, inadequately documented, or necessary to operate the company. A lender may apply its own rules. The relevant question is not simply “What can be added back?” but “What cash flow can a qualified reviewer support as transferable and recurring?”

Keep a schedule showing:

- Reported amount and accounting period
- Proposed adjustment
- Business reason
- Source document
- Whether the item is recurring
- Who reviewed it
- Buyer or lender treatment, if known

## How multiples enter the conversation

Market participants may discuss a multiple of SDE, EBITDA, revenue, or another industry measure. A multiple is not a law of nature. It is a shorthand that reflects risk, expected growth, transferability, deal structure, market conditions, buyer type, and the quality of the underlying metric.

Revenue multiples can be relevant in some recurring-revenue, technology, digital, or high-growth models, but revenue alone does not show margin, retention, capital needs, customer quality, or the cost of delivery. Applying a familiar multiple to the wrong metric—or to weak inputs—creates false precision.

Do not prescribe a universal multiple. If market transactions are used, examine comparability: size, geography, industry, business model, customer concentration, growth, margin, date, ownership interest, and transaction terms. Headline announcements may omit seller notes, earnouts, rollover equity, retained working capital, or assumed liabilities.

## Cash-flow quality and durability

Buyers price uncertainty. Two businesses with similar historical earnings can attract different value conclusions because one has clearer records, stronger recurrence, less concentration, more capable management, and better evidence of future performance.

Common cash-flow questions include:

- Are monthly financial statements timely and reconciled?
- Do tax returns, management accounts, and bank records align?
- What portion of revenue is recurring, contracted, repeat, or project-based?
- How stable are gross margin and operating margin?
- Is backlog enforceable and likely to convert?
- What customer or channel losses would materially affect earnings?
- Are there deferred maintenance, hiring, compliance, or technology costs?
- What working capital is normally required to operate?

Recurring revenue can improve visibility, but the label must be supported. Separate contractual recurrence, auto-renewal, repeat purchasing, subscriptions, service agreements, and one-time project revenue. Report retention and concentration only when the period and source are clear.

## Concentration, dependence, and transferability

Customer concentration can increase risk when a small number of relationships account for a material portion of revenue or margin. The effect depends on contract quality, relationship tenure, renewal behavior, switching costs, customer health, and whether the relationship depends on the seller.

Owner dependence is another major issue. If the owner controls sales, pricing, technical delivery, customer trust, vendor terms, and every important exception, the buyer may need a longer transition, replacement hires, contingent payments, or a lower risk-adjusted price. Read [Reducing Owner Dependence](/resources/reducing-owner-dependence) before assuming historical cash flow will transfer unchanged.

Transferability also includes contracts, leases, permits, licenses, systems, intellectual property, workforce, and operational knowledge. The economic value of an asset is limited if the buyer cannot legally or practically use it after closing.

## Growth and risk

Growth can support value when it is demonstrated, explainable, and feasible. Separate historical performance, signed backlog, qualified pipeline, tested channels, available capacity, and management aspirations. A plan that requires unproven pricing, heavy capital, new licenses, or talent not yet hired should be labeled accordingly.

Risk analysis should include:

- Revenue volatility and concentration
- Workforce and key-person exposure
- Legal, regulatory, environmental, or licensing issues
- Cybersecurity, data, and platform dependence
- Supplier, landlord, and channel dependencies
- Deferred capital expenditures
- Working-capital swings
- Unresolved disputes, liens, guarantees, or insurance claims
- Technology obsolescence and intellectual-property ownership

Documenting risk does not automatically reduce value. It makes the issue reviewable and gives the owner a chance to resolve, allocate, or price it deliberately.

## Working capital, equipment, and real estate

Transaction value is not always the same as the cash a seller receives at closing. A purchase-price discussion may assume a normal level of working capital, exclude cash, require debt payoff, treat inventory separately, or adjust for capital expenditure needs. Equipment may be included, leased, financed, obsolete, or worth more or less than its accounting value.

Real estate can be sold with the operating business, retained and leased, or handled separately. Each choice changes cash flow, risk, financing, and tax considerations. Use qualified valuation, legal, tax, accounting, and financing professionals to analyze these components.

## Listing price versus transaction value

A listing price is an asking position. Transaction value reflects negotiated economics. Net seller proceeds depend on structure, debt payoff, working capital, transaction expenses, taxes, holdbacks, escrows, seller notes, earnouts, rollover equity, and post-close obligations.

When comparing offers, create a structure table showing cash at close, contingent value, timing, security, working-capital treatment, retained liabilities, transition requirements, and conditions. This makes a lower-risk offer visible even when its headline number is not the highest.

## Practical preparation checklist

- Clarify the valuation purpose and intended users.
- Reconcile three years of financial statements and current interim results.
- Build a documented adjustment schedule.
- Separate SDE, EBITDA, and revenue metrics; do not mix their multiples.
- Prepare customer, channel, product, and geography concentration analyses.
- Document recurring-revenue definitions and retention periods.
- Map owner and key-person dependence.
- Identify working-capital norms and capital expenditure requirements.
- Inventory equipment, leases, real estate, and non-operating assets.
- Record growth evidence separately from projections.
- Obtain professional review for the purpose that requires it.

## Questions to ask a valuation professional

- What standard and premise of value apply to this purpose?
- Which methods are appropriate, and why?
- How are owner compensation and proposed add-backs treated?
- What evidence supports the cash-flow forecast?
- How are concentration, transferability, and working capital reflected?
- Are transaction terms embedded in the market data?
- What limitations should every reader understand?

## Educational boundary

This guide is educational and does not constitute a valuation, appraisal, fairness opinion, legal, tax, accounting, brokerage, investment, or financing conclusion. Value depends on purpose, evidence, date, rights, assumptions, and professional judgment.
