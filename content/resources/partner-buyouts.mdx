## Executive overview

A partner buyout can look simpler than an external sale because the parties know the company. The relationship can make it harder: each owner has access, history, expectations, and leverage. A disciplined process separates the operating business from negotiation and protects employees, customers, and cash flow.

## Start with the ownership agreement

Collect formation documents, operating or shareholder agreements, buy-sell provisions, amendments, side letters, employment agreements, debt documents, and prior valuation materials. Ask counsel to identify notice requirements, transfer restrictions, triggers, valuation mechanisms, payment terms, rights of first refusal, deadlock procedures, and required approvals.

Do not assume a historic formula still produces the intended result or is enforceable for the current event.

## Define scope and decision authority

Identify the interests being transferred, included rights, effective date, treatment of distributions, owner loans, accrued compensation, retained assets, real estate, and continuing obligations. Establish who can bind the company during negotiations and how conflicts will be managed.

Use a written process calendar. Continue ordinary operations under agreed authority limits. Avoid using customer, employee, or vendor decisions as negotiation pressure.

## Use a fit-for-purpose valuation process

The agreement may specify a formula, appraisal process, negotiation, or another mechanism. Qualified legal and valuation professionals should interpret and perform the relevant work.

Define treatment of:

- Debt and cash
- Working capital
- Owner compensation
- Related-party rent and expenses
- Personal goodwill where relevant
- Minority or control rights
- Real estate and non-operating assets
- Contingent liabilities

Read [Understanding Business Valuation](/resources/understanding-business-valuation). A preliminary estimate is not a formal conclusion.

## Build the funding plan

Funding can come from buyer cash, company redemption, acquisition debt, seller financing, insurance proceeds where applicable, or combinations. Each affects the company, the remaining owner, creditors, and taxes.

Prepare sources and uses plus post-closing cash needs. Review covenants, lender consent, distribution restrictions, and solvency considerations with professionals. A buyout should not leave the operating company unable to fund payroll, working capital, taxes, maintenance, or growth.

If the seller accepts a note, review [Seller Financing](/resources/seller-financing) and document security, subordination, payment restrictions, reporting, default, and administration.

## Resolve debt and personal guarantees

Ownership transfer does not automatically release a departing owner from personal guarantees. Inventory bank debt, leases, cards, equipment financing, bonding, landlord guarantees, and vendor credit.

For each obligation, record:

- Obligor and guarantor
- Current balance and maturity
- Collateral
- Consent or notice requirement
- Proposed payoff, assumption, refinance, or release
- Written confirmation status

Do not treat a promised release as complete until the responsible counterparty documents it.

## Clarify control and distributions

Define the effective transfer of voting, banking, contracts, systems, records, and authority. Address distributions before and after the effective date, tax distributions, retained earnings, owner expenses, and final payroll.

If the departing owner retains equity or a note, specify information rights and reserved matters. Continuing rights should not unintentionally block normal operations or leave the seller exposed without visibility.

## Plan the transition

Document customer and vendor introductions, technical knowledge, passwords and systems, licenses, employee matters, open projects, disputes, and owner-specific relationships. Set a time-limited support period with defined scope and compensation.

Read [What Happens After Closing](/resources/what-happens-after-closing) for post-close administration.

## Manage deadlock and confidentiality

Use the agreement’s process and qualified counsel when owners cannot agree. Keep negotiations within an authorized group. Limit identifying or sensitive disclosures to necessary parties and maintain an audit trail.

Employees and customers should hear a coordinated message only when timing and facts are ready. Rumor can damage the value both owners are trying to protect.

## Reconcile owner accounts and historical practices

Partner-owned businesses often contain informal arrangements that become material during a separation: personal expenses, owner loans, uneven distributions, related-party rent, vehicles, insurance, benefits, accumulated tax obligations, or unrecorded commitments.

Create a reconciliation schedule with the original source, accounting treatment, each owner’s position, professional analysis, and proposed closing treatment. Do not resolve a disputed item by quietly changing the books. Preserve the original record and document any authorized adjustment.

Review whether historical practices must continue through closing and who can approve deviations. A last-minute distribution, bonus, customer concession, or capital expenditure can change working capital or create another dispute.

## Compare entity purchase and redemption paths

Depending on the business and governing documents, the remaining owner may purchase the interest, the company may redeem it, or another structure may be considered. The legal, tax, cash-flow, creditor, governance, and accounting consequences differ.

Ask qualified professionals to compare:

- Who pays and who receives the interest
- Effect on company cash and leverage
- Treatment of basis, taxes, and transaction costs
- Required consents and solvency considerations
- Continuing indemnities and representations
- Effect on remaining ownership and governance
- Security for deferred payments

Do not choose a label before the facts and professional analysis are available.

## Prepare the closing and post-close handoff

Build a closing matrix for signatures, funds, debt payoffs, releases, resignations, governance appointments, tax forms, records, access, notices, and employee or customer communications. Identify which items become effective at signing, funding, or another agreed time.

After closing, calendar seller-note payments, information rights, indemnity periods, escrow claims, tax cooperation, retained records, and any transition support. If the departing partner retains a role, define it in writing and prevent conflicting authority.

## Questions to ask the professional team

- Which agreement provisions control this event?
- What valuation process and standard apply?
- Which owner-account items require reconciliation?
- How will existing debt and guarantees be released or replaced?
- What structure preserves operating liquidity?
- Which consents and notices are closing conditions?
- What obligations continue after the ownership transfer?

## Practical checklist

- Complete ownership-document set
- Defined trigger and process
- Valuation scope and professional
- Sources and uses
- Working-capital impact
- Debt and guarantee release schedule
- Control-transfer matrix
- Distribution and owner-account reconciliation
- Seller-note terms where applicable
- Confidentiality and communication plan
- Legal and tax review

## A 30-day issue-resolution plan

1. Preserve the governing documents and original financial records.
2. Ask counsel to identify the controlling process and immediate duties.
3. Create the owner-account and guarantee schedules.
4. Define ordinary-course decision limits during negotiation.
5. Select the valuation process and required evidence.
6. Build at least two funding scenarios with working-capital impact.
7. List every required lender, landlord, regulator, or third-party consent.
8. Draft the control, access, and communication handoff.
9. Establish a confidential issue log and meeting cadence.

If the relationship is strained, use structured communication and qualified advisors. Do not let operational employees become messengers between owners.

Maintain a decision record for every agreed item. It should name the issue, source documents, alternatives considered, responsible advisor, owner approval, financial effect, dependency, and final treatment. This reduces the chance that a preliminary conversation is later mistaken for a binding agreement and gives the closing team one reliable source of truth.

Review it before every formal approval.
