## Executive overview

A management buyout can preserve operating continuity by transferring ownership to leaders who already know the company. Familiarity is an advantage, not proof of readiness. The team must demonstrate leadership capability, decision authority, financial capacity, governance discipline, and a credible plan for the owner’s responsibilities after closing.

The owner should compare the management path with external sale, family succession, partner transfer, and continued ownership. Evaluate control, liquidity, timing, employee and customer continuity, seller risk, financing, and professional-review burden.

## Define the buyer group

Clarify who is actually buying. A broad group of interested managers is not yet a buyer entity. Identify proposed ownership percentages, capital contributions, roles, voting rights, economic rights, and the process for adding or removing participants.

Discuss difficult scenarios early:

- One manager cannot fund the expected contribution
- A participant leaves before closing
- The group disagrees on valuation or strategy
- A key leader wants management authority without ownership
- Ownership percentages conflict with operating responsibility
- One member becomes disabled, dies, or breaches an obligation

Keep employee information confidential and involve counsel before making promises.

## Assess leadership readiness

Evaluate the team against the work the owner performs today. Can management own sales, customer relationships, pricing, technical delivery, finance, people, vendors, quality, and exceptions? Does it understand cash flow, working capital, debt service, and capital allocation?

Use evidence:

- Performance against operating metrics
- Decision quality during owner absences
- Customer and employee retention
- Budget ownership
- Hiring and performance-management experience
- Strategic planning and execution
- Integrity of internal reporting

Create development milestones for gaps. A title or long tenure does not replace tested authority.

## Establish an independent valuation process

The seller and managers have overlapping relationships and conflicting economic interests. Use a clear valuation scope and qualified professional where appropriate. Define what is being valued, the date, purpose, assumptions, and treatment of working capital, debt, real estate, and related-party arrangements.

Read [Understanding Business Valuation](/resources/understanding-business-valuation). Avoid using an informal internal estimate as a binding conclusion.

## Build the capital structure

Potential sources can include management equity, acquisition debt, seller financing, earnouts, rollover equity, or staged ownership. Each source affects cash flow, control, seller risk, and governance.

Model sources and uses plus post-closing cash needs. The company still needs payroll, working capital, maintenance, insurance, and growth capital. A structure that pays the seller but starves the business is not a durable transition.

Read [Seller Financing](/resources/seller-financing) and [SBA Acquisition Financing](/resources/sba-acquisition-financing). Financing scenarios remain educational until lenders and qualified advisors review current facts.

## Decide the seller’s transition role

Define duration, hours, responsibilities, authority, compensation, customer introductions, employee communication, and the endpoint. Avoid “the seller will help as needed.” That phrase creates mismatched expectations and can undermine new leadership.

Use milestones:

1. Pre-close delegation and management testing
2. Day-one authority transfer
3. Customer and vendor introductions
4. Time-limited operating support
5. Advisory availability under written boundaries
6. Complete handoff

## Design governance

New owners need rules for budgets, distributions, debt, compensation, hiring, capital expenditures, related-party transactions, major contracts, deadlock, transfer restrictions, and future exits. Operating roles and ownership rights should be distinct.

Use qualified legal and tax advisors to create the entity, governing agreements, employment arrangements, buy-sell provisions, and decision rules. Make reporting requirements visible to sellers who retain notes or equity.

## Manage conflicts and employee communication

Managers negotiate as buyers while remaining employees and fiduciaries or agents in existing roles. Define information access, negotiation authority, confidentiality, expense approval, and recusal where appropriate. Obtain legal guidance.

Do not announce the buyout until the transaction and communication plan are ready. Key employees may worry about job security, leadership conflict, or favoritism. [Preparing Employees and Customers for a Transition](/resources/preparing-employees-and-customers) provides a sequencing framework.

## Build a phased diligence process

Management already knows the company operationally, but it should not skip transaction diligence. Familiarity can conceal assumptions about ownership, liabilities, contracts, taxes, working capital, technology, and employee obligations. The buyer group needs an authorized record set and independent professional review.

Create workstreams for financial, legal, tax, commercial, operational, technology, workforce, insurance, and financing diligence. Assign one owner to each request and preserve source documents separately from management summaries. If managers prepared information in their employee roles, document the authority and avoid using confidential records outside the approved buyout process.

The seller should receive a concise issue log rather than uncontrolled questions from multiple team members. Each item should show the fact, source, financial or operating effect, proposed resolution, responsible party, and closing dependency.

## Compare structure alternatives

Do not force every issue into the purchase price. Compare a direct purchase, staged acquisition, partial sale, seller-financed transition, or other professionally designed path against:

- Seller liquidity and retained risk
- Management equity and debt capacity
- Operating cash needs
- Control and voting rights
- Timing and closing dependencies
- Tax and legal review
- Employee and customer continuity
- Ability to unwind if milestones fail

An earnout can bridge different expectations, but it creates measurement and operating-control questions. Rollover equity can preserve upside, but it makes the seller a continuing investor with minority-rights and future-liquidity concerns. Use written scenarios with assumptions rather than relying on labels.

## A 90-day management-buyout plan

1. Establish the buyer group and confidentiality protocol.
2. Complete individual leadership and capital-capacity assessments.
3. Define an independent valuation and diligence process.
4. Build sources and uses plus a post-close operating budget.
5. Compare at least two feasible transaction structures.
6. Draft governance, seller-role, and conflict issues for counsel.
7. Test management authority during a planned owner absence.
8. Prepare employee and customer communication triggers.
9. Identify lender, legal, tax, accounting, and valuation decision gates.

## A practical readiness checklist

- Named buyer group and proposed ownership
- Leadership gap assessment
- Independent value-review process
- Documented buyer equity
- Preliminary sources and uses
- Working-capital plan
- Seller-role milestones
- Governance and deadlock framework
- Confidentiality and conflict protocol
- Employee and customer communication plan
- Legal, tax, accounting, valuation, and financing review

## Questions to ask the buyer group

- Which responsibilities will each manager own on day one?
- What personal capital is documented and actually available?
- How will the group make decisions before governance documents are final?
- Which leadership gaps require hiring, training, or seller support?
- What happens if one participant withdraws before closing?
- Which financial assumptions create the most repayment risk?
- How will compensation and distributions be separated?
- What information does the seller retain if a note or rollover interest continues?
- Which milestone would cause the parties to pause the process?

Answers should be written, compared, and tested against the operating plan. Agreement in a meeting is not enough when capital, control, and careers are at stake.

Repeat the review before signing and again before closing.

Assign one person to maintain the approved record.
