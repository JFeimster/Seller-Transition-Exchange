## Executive overview

Selling a business is not one decision. It is a sequence of decisions about timing, price expectations, confidentiality, buyer fit, risk allocation, and what happens to the people and relationships that helped build the company. Owners who prepare early can make those decisions while they still have room to improve the business. Owners who wait until a buyer is already asking questions often have to respond under pressure.

> A strong exit begins before the listing goes live.

Preparation does not mean predicting every transaction term. It means clarifying the result you want, establishing an honest evidence baseline, reducing preventable uncertainty, and designing a process you can control. The work is especially valuable twelve to twenty-four months before a possible sale, but it remains useful when buyer conversations have already started.

## Define the owner’s objective

Start with the outcome, not the marketing materials. A headline price matters, but it is only one part of the transition. Write down what the sale must accomplish for you and what you would prefer it to protect.

- Desired timing and the reasons behind it
- Minimum liquidity needs and acceptable payment risk
- Willingness to finance part of the purchase price
- Preferred post-closing role, if any
- Priorities for employees, customers, brand, community, and family
- Appetite for a competitive process versus a private mandate
- Preference for an individual buyer, management team, family successor, strategic buyer, or financial buyer
- Conditions that would cause you to pause or decline a transaction

Rank these priorities. Tradeoffs are normal. A buyer offering the highest headline price may ask for a longer earnout, more seller financing, more restrictive representations, or a transition role you do not want. The highest headline price is not always the strongest transition.

## Establish the timeline and readiness baseline

Build the timeline backward from a range rather than a single closing date. Include time for improvement work, advisor selection, documentation, buyer outreach, diligence, financing, definitive agreements, closing conditions, and post-close support. Financing and third-party approvals can affect timing, and no preparation schedule guarantees a close.

Use the [Exit Readiness assessment](/exit-readiness) to evaluate financial record quality, revenue durability, customer concentration, owner dependence, management depth, operating procedures, legal organization, transferability, and transition planning. Treat the result as a directional preparation measure—not a valuation, probability of sale, or promise of financing.

The baseline should separate:

- Evidence that is current and available
- Seller statements that still require documentation
- Missing information
- Conflicting records
- Risks that need professional review
- Improvements that can realistically be completed before market entry

Do not hide a weakness from yourself because you plan to disclose it later. Buyers price uncertainty, and unresolved uncertainty tends to become leverage during diligence.

## Prepare the financial story

Buyers need to understand how the business makes money and how reliably that performance can continue after ownership changes. Assemble monthly and annual profit-and-loss statements, balance sheets, tax returns, bank records, debt schedules, payroll information, accounts receivable and payable, inventory records where applicable, and a history of material capital expenditures.

Reconcile differences before presenting the package. If management statements, tax returns, and bank activity tell different stories, document the reason and the responsible reviewer. Identify owner compensation, personal or discretionary expenses, nonrecurring items, and proposed add-backs, but do not assume a buyer or lender will accept every adjustment.

Read [Organizing Financial Records for Buyers and Lenders](/resources/organizing-financial-records) for a detailed preparation sequence. Clean records shorten expensive conversations.

## Test transferability

A buyer is purchasing an operating system, not only a stream of historical earnings. Map the functions the owner currently performs: selling, estimating, pricing, technical delivery, hiring, scheduling, vendor management, quality control, banking, and exception handling. For each function, identify who can perform it today, what authority they have, and what documentation supports them.

Review:

- Leadership coverage and decision rights
- Standard operating procedures for recurring and exception processes
- Customer relationships tied personally to the owner
- Supplier arrangements and informal commitments
- Employee retention risks and key-person dependencies
- Contracts, leases, licenses, permits, and change-of-control provisions
- Passwords, systems, domains, intellectual property, and access ownership
- Insurance coverage and unresolved claims

Use [Reducing Owner Dependence](/resources/reducing-owner-dependence) to create a delegation and management-development plan. A business that cannot operate without its owner is harder to transfer.

## Organize the legal and operating record

Create a controlled index before uploading files anywhere. The index should show the document name, period, source, owner, verification status, confidentiality level, and last update. Keep original records separate from cleaned summaries. A summary is useful, but it should never overwrite the underlying evidence.

Common categories include:

- Formation, ownership, and governance documents
- Material customer and vendor contracts
- Leases, licenses, permits, and regulatory records
- Employee agreements, policies, benefit plans, and contractor arrangements
- Intellectual-property registrations and assignments
- Insurance policies and claims history
- Litigation, disputes, liens, and compliance matters
- Financial statements, tax records, and operating reports

Ask a transaction attorney, CPA, and other relevant professionals to identify gaps within their scope. Not every business or transaction needs the same advisor team, but material legal, tax, accounting, valuation, and financing decisions require qualified review.

## Design confidentiality as a workflow

Confidentiality begins before outreach. Decide what can be public, what can appear in an anonymous teaser, what is limited to verified buyers, and what requires explicit seller approval. Remove direct identifiers and combinations of facts that could reveal the company.

Buyer verification, an NDA, and seller approval are separate gates. An NDA does not automatically justify full disclosure. Use staged access: anonymous summary, qualified overview, financial package, and controlled deal-room materials as the buyer demonstrates fit and the seller approves the scope.

Read [Confidential Business Sales](/resources/confidential-business-sales) and use the [Seller Launch Studio](/seller-launch-studio) to prepare review-required materials. Confidentiality is a workflow—not a checkbox.

## Define the buyer profile and transaction preferences

Describe the buyer most likely to operate the business successfully and complete the transaction. Consider industry experience, operating capability, geography, acquisition experience, available equity, financing readiness, timeline, culture, and willingness to honor transition priorities.

Also document preliminary preferences for an asset or equity transaction, full or partial sale, seller note, earnout, rollover equity, transition period, working-capital treatment, and retained real estate. These are discussion inputs, not final legal or tax conclusions. Preserve flexibility until qualified advisors and actual counterparties can evaluate the facts.

## A 30-day preparation plan

1. Write a one-page seller objective memo and rank the non-price priorities.
2. Complete the Exit Readiness assessment using current evidence.
3. Build a document index and identify the five most important missing items.
4. Reconcile the latest financial statements with bank and tax records.
5. Map every recurring function that still depends on the owner.
6. Define the initial confidentiality level and disclosure approvals.
7. List candidate advisors and the exact scope each one would cover.

## A 90-day preparation plan

1. Resolve the highest-impact financial inconsistencies.
2. Delegate two material owner-dependent functions with documented authority.
3. Complete critical SOPs and a management coverage map.
4. Review transfer restrictions in material contracts, leases, and licenses.
5. Draft an evidence-grounded business overview and anonymous teaser.
6. Define buyer qualification criteria and a controlled outreach plan.
7. Review the likely tax, legal, valuation, and financing gates with qualified professionals.
8. Decide whether the business is ready to package, needs a value-building period, or should pause.

## Questions to ask an advisor

- Which preparation issue is most likely to delay diligence?
- Which financial adjustments are supportable, and what evidence is required?
- What information should remain withheld until a later disclosure gate?
- Which contracts or approvals could affect transferability?
- What transaction structures deserve review based on the owner’s objectives?
- What assumptions in the seller’s value expectations require independent analysis?
- What work must be completed before buyer outreach begins?
